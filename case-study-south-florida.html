The project

A three-story single-family residence in South Florida — reinforced concrete slab construction with concrete beams of varying depth, 14′-0″ floor-to-floor at the first and second levels. The drawing set ran 63 sheets across Architectural, Structural, MEP, and Civil disciplines. The set was signed, sealed, and out for bid.

The issue Flikt.AI flagged — before a shovel hit the ground

Flikt.AI's plan coordination pass surfaced a Mechanical / Structural spatial clash in the first- and second-floor ceiling cavities. The finding is reproduced below from the Plan Coordination Report delivered to the project team:

Description
The mechanical plans show supply and return ducts on the first level (M-2) with sizes up to 26×10 and 20×8 (10 inches tall), and on the second level (M-3) with sizes up to 24×8 and 20×8. The structure is reinforced concrete slab construction with concrete beams (structural sections S-4.0 through S-4.7 show 8″ concrete slabs with concrete beams of various depths). The architectural sections (A-10, A-12) show the floor-to-floor height from understory to second level is 14′-0″, and from second level to roof is +24′-8″ to +26′-0″. With concrete beams dropping below the slab soffit, the available plenum space for duct routing is constrained. The 10-inch-tall ducts (26×10) must pass below or between concrete beams. No coordination sections are provided showing duct clearance relative to beam depths.
Recommended Action
Provide MEP coordination sections at critical locations where large ducts cross structural beams. Verify that duct heights (8–10 inches) plus insulation plus clearance can fit below concrete beam soffits while maintaining required ceiling heights. Consider reducing duct heights and increasing widths where beam conflicts occur.

This was finding C005 in an 11-item Plan Coordination Report — 8 High, 2 Medium, 1 Low — generated from the issued-for-construction set.

What was built on site

The clash was not caught during shop drawing review, was not picked up in the field, and was not raised as an RFI. The duct ran. The beam was where the beam was always going to be. By the time the conflict became visible, the only resolution available was a field-built interior soffit to conceal the duct dropping below the structural beam soffit — a permanent reduction in finished ceiling height in the affected rooms, plus material, framing, drywall, finish, and re-inspection.

Why the field missed it

Three things make this kind of clash invisible to a manual review:

  1. It lives between disciplines. The mechanical sheets show duct size and routing. The structural sheets show beam depth and slab thickness. The architectural sections show floor-to-floor heights. None of the three sheets, looked at alone, shows the conflict — and there were no MEP coordination sections drawn.
  2. It's a clearance problem, not a hard hit. Duct can pass through the plenum. It just can't pass through the plenum and maintain the architect's ceiling heights and clear the deepest beams. That's the math a reviewer has to do across three disciplines simultaneously.
  3. It survives sign-off because every individual sheet is correct. The mechanical engineer sized the duct correctly. The structural engineer sized the beams correctly. The architect dimensioned the section correctly. The coordination gap is in the white space between drawings.

Flikt.AI reads the white space. That's the job.

The counterfactual

Had the C005 recommendation been issued as an RFI before MEP rough-in:

  • Duct heights could have been reduced (8″ tall × wider) where beam conflicts occurred — a sheet revision, not a field event.
  • The interior soffit would not have been built.
  • The 2-week delay disappears.
  • The $10,000+ doesn't get spent.

Flikt.AI flagged the issue, named the sheets, and gave a cost range that ended up bracketing the actual overrun. The cost of the Flikt.AI pass was a small fraction of what was spent fixing the same problem in the field.
